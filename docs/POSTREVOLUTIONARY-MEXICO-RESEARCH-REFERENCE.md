# Executive summary

The recommended first historical arc is **1917-1940**, with a compact prelude on 1910-1917. Although the original idea begins around 1918, 1917 is the more useful analytical threshold because the new Constitution supplies recurring legal and political reference points, while 1920 marks a second threshold in the collapse of Carranza's government and the rise of the Sonoran leadership. The endpoint, 1940, closes the Cárdenas presidency and a major phase of postrevolutionary reform and state-building; it does not imply that the Revolution's conflicts were resolved.

> How did Mexico transform a many-sided revolution into a government, a national culture, and a durable political order - and what happened to the Revolution's promises?

A strong first version would use a twelve-chapter progression organized into three acts: unresolved revolution; construction and contestation of revolutionary Mexico; and institutionalization, mass politics, and Cardenismo. The sequence should not become a list of presidents. Each chapter should move among national institutions, regional and local experience, social actors, cultural production, and international pressures.

The recurring analytical structure is **promise -> policy -> implementation -> local response -> beneficiaries and exclusions -> memory or legacy**. Six promises can anchor a cumulative ledger: land, labor, education, secular government, political participation, and national sovereignty. Cross-cutting lenses should include gender and citizenship, Indigenous politics, regional diversity, cultural memory, state capacity, and U.S.-Mexico relations.

Spanish should be embedded in the historical work rather than added as a separate course. A typical chapter should actively teach only five to eight words or expressions, one historically useful concept word, one grammar or rhetorical pattern, one short listening activity, and one brief Spanish output. Complex historical interpretation may remain in English while a smaller part of the same idea is expressed in Spanish. Original text, glossed text, contemporary-Spanish paraphrase, and English translation must remain visibly distinct.

The two strongest prototype chapters are the opening chapter on the 1917 Constitution and the culminating chapter on the 1938 petroleum expropriation. Together they test legal and institutional source work, regional scaling, speeches and listening, labor and corporate perspectives, national rhetoric, and the cumulative promise-to-outcome framework.

# 2. Historical scope, audience, and central questions

## 2.1 Recommended first arc: 1917-1940

The exploration can be described to the user as beginning 'around 1918,' but the recommended content architecture starts in **1917**. The Constitution promulgated on 5 February 1917 created enduring reference points for education, land and subsoil resources, labor, and Church-state relations. It did not end the armed and political struggle. Zapata was killed in 1919; Carranza's government fell in 1920; and the meaning of constitutional promises remained contested. [P1; P2; C1]

The year **1920** should function as a second threshold rather than as an absolute end of the Revolution. The Plan of Agua Prieta, the collapse of Carranza's coalition, the provisional government of Adolfo de la Huerta, and the accession of Alvaro Obregón changed the governing coalition while leaving military networks, regional power, and popular demands central to politics. [P4; S1; S8]

The year **1940** is an effective stopping point for a first season or volume. It closes the Cárdenas presidency after major agrarian, labor, educational, party, and petroleum developments. It should be presented as a transition, not as closure. Later questions about industrialization, migration, corporatism, electoral authority, urbanization, repression, 1968, market reform, the 1994 Zapatista uprising, and the end of uninterrupted PRI presidential rule belong to later arcs.

## 2.2 Recommended central question

> How did Mexico transform a many-sided revolution into a government, a national culture, and a durable political order - and what happened to the Revolution's promises?

This question gives the exploration a stronger intellectual spine than a president-by-president chronology. It allows the user to examine reform and control, participation and incorporation, national policy and local negotiation, culture and administration, and domestic change and foreign pressure as parts of the same historical problem.

## 2.3 Supporting questions

- How does a revolution end, and who has the authority to say that it has ended?
- When does constitutional recognition become practical implementation?
- Did stronger institutions reduce personal and military rule, reorganize it, or do both?
- How did policies change when they moved from Mexico City into particular states, villages, workplaces, and religious communities?
- How did schools, murals, public ceremonies, newspapers, and political language construct a shared version of revolutionary Mexico?
- How did ordinary people use, reinterpret, negotiate, or resist revolutionary institutions?
- Could land redistribution, labor organization, secular education, and national sovereignty advance social reform while also strengthening presidential and party authority?
- Which groups gained resources, voice, or recognition, and whose claims were postponed, absorbed, or suppressed?

## 2.4 Audience implications

The intended user is highly educated and accustomed to long-form historical narration. The material should therefore assume intellectual maturity without assuming specialist knowledge of Mexican institutions, regional geography, legal Spanish, or the historiography. The experience can sustain complex causation, competing interpretations, long audio segments, and primary-source ambiguity. It should avoid elementary quiz structures and unnecessary simplification.

The main challenge is not keeping the user busy. It is helping the user maintain orientation across many actors and scales while preserving the ability to investigate evidence in depth. A persistent timeline, map, actor and institution reference, promise ledger, and evolving thesis would support this kind of user particularly well.

## 2.5 Recommended learning outcomes for the first arc

- Explain the principal political transitions from Carranza through Cárdenas without reducing the period to a succession of presidents.
- Trace land, labor, education, secularism, political participation, and sovereignty from constitutional or revolutionary promise through uneven implementation.
- Distinguish national law and rhetoric from regional and local experience.
- Interpret laws, speeches, petitions, newspapers, photographs, murals, reports, and testimony as situated evidence.
- Explain why reform and state-building can be mutually reinforcing rather than mutually exclusive.
- Recognize that the later ruling-party system was historically produced rather than inevitable.
- Form a defensible, qualified answer to the central question by 1940.

# 3. Recurring themes, regions, actors, and interpretive safeguards

## 3.1 Repeating analytical model

A strong recurring structure is **promise -> policy -> implementation -> local response -> beneficiaries and exclusions -> memory or legacy**. The user should repeatedly see that a constitutional provision, decree, party program, or presidential speech establishes an intention and a claim to authority; it does not by itself establish what occurred throughout Mexico.

| Stage | Recurring question |
| --- | --- |
| Promise | What social, political, or constitutional claim was made, and by whom? |
| Policy | What law, institution, program, alliance, or administrative mechanism was created? |
| Implementation | What capacity, money, personnel, information, and coercive authority were available? |
| Local response | How did governors, municipalities, communities, workplaces, churches, teachers, or military actors alter the policy? |
| Distribution | Who gained land, rights, recognition, organization, or access? Who bore costs or remained excluded? |
| Memory | How was the event later incorporated into official, regional, popular, or oppositional accounts of the Revolution? |

## 3.2 Core promises and cross-cutting lenses

| Thread | Questions to carry across chapters |
| --- | --- |
| Land | How did Article 27, village claims, hacienda power, ejidos, and regional politics shape redistribution? |
| Labor | How did constitutional labor protections, unions, state mediation, strikes, and political incorporation interact? |
| Education | Were schools primarily public services, nation-building institutions, cultural interventions, or contested local spaces? |
| Secular government | How did constitutional rules, anticlerical enforcement, Catholic practice, education, and local authority collide? |
| Political participation | Did new institutions widen participation, channel it, constrain competition, or all three? |
| National sovereignty | How did resource ownership, foreign recognition, petroleum, debt, and U.S. relations shape policy? |
| State capacity | What could the central government actually administer, finance, document, or enforce at different moments? |
| Gender and citizenship | How did women participate as teachers, workers, organizers, Catholics, peasants, and political actors despite restricted formal citizenship? |
| Indigenous politics | How did national projects interpret Indigenous identity, language, land, and authority, and how did communities respond? |
| Culture and memory | How did murals, photography, corridos, schoolbooks, ceremonies, film, and later commemoration narrate the Revolution? |

| Michoacan | Cardenismo, agrarian politics, Catholic culture, and the relationship between reformers and peasant communities. |
| Yucatan | Regional socialism, Maya politics, henequen labor, women’s organizing, and the limits of Cardenista reform. |
| La Laguna | Collective agrarian reform, labor and peasant organization, and women’s political mobilization. |

## 3.4 Recurring actors and institutions

| Category | Examples to track |
| --- | --- |
| National political leaders | Venustiano Carranza, Emiliano Zapata, Alvaro Obregón, Plutarco Elias Calles, Adolfo de la Huerta, Emilio Portes Gil, Pascual Ortiz Rubio, Abelardo L. Rodriguez, Lazaro Cárdenas. |
| Military and regional power | Generals, state governors, local bosses or caciques, revolutionary veterans, federal troops, Cristero forces. |
| Federal institutions | Presidency, Congress, courts, agrarian agencies, Secretaría de Educación Pública, labor authorities, military, diplomatic service. |
| Political organizations | Regional parties, PNR, PRM, opposition campaigns, labor and peasant confederations. |
| Communities and social groups | Village and communal authorities, ejidatarios, hacendados, workers, teachers, students, women’s organizations, Indigenous communities, Catholics, clergy, lay associations. |
| International actors | United States government, diplomats, creditors, petroleum companies, foreign investors, transnational workers and migrants. |

## 3.5 Interpretive safeguards

- Do not present the Revolution as a single movement with one agreed program. Constitutionalists, Zapatistas, Villistas, regional socialists, labor groups, Catholic actors, communities, and military leaders often meant different things by revolutionary legitimacy.
- Do not use 'the government' as if the presidency, governors, municipal authorities, teachers, judges, the army, and party organizations always acted coherently.
- Do not treat peasants, Indigenous people, workers, Catholics, women, or regional elites as internally uniform categories.
- Do not equate legal enactment with implementation, or the presence of an institution with effective control.
- Do not assume that state-building was only repression or only progress. Schools, unions, agrarian agencies, and parties could distribute resources and voice while also channeling participation and displacing local authority.
- Do not describe the PRI as if it already existed throughout the 1920s and 1930s. Use **PNR** for 1929-1938, **PRM** for 1938-1946, and **PRI** only from 1946.
- Do not use a modern amended constitutional text as if it were the exact text promulgated in 1917. Store and cite the original version separately from later amendments.
- Do not treat later outcomes as inevitable. Succession crises, rebellions, regional political experiments, assassinations, and opposition campaigns should remain visible.
- Do not use a national aggregate to erase regional variation, or one regional case to claim that the same outcome occurred throughout Mexico.
- Do not gamify persecution, armed conflict, execution, displacement, or repression. Activities involving difficult events should focus on evidence, constraint, testimony, and interpretation.

# 4. Recommended twelve-chapter historical progression

The following progression is a recommendation, not a mandatory chapter list. Its value is the cumulative argument: each chapter changes how the user understands revolutionary legitimacy, state capacity, local negotiation, and the relationship between reform and political order.

## Act I. Revolution without resolution

### Chapter 1. 1917: A Constitution, Not a Conclusion

**Role in the arc.** Establish the Constitution as a legal framework, political program, and contested promise rather than as proof that the Revolution had ended.

**Central question.** Did the Constitution of 1917 complete the Revolution, or create a framework for struggles that remained unresolved?

**Historical backbone.**

- A compact prelude from the Porfirian order through Madero, the overthrow of Madero, and the conflicts among Constitutionalists, Zapatistas, Villistas, and other forces.
- Promulgation of the Constitution on 5 February 1917 and its entry into force later that year.
- Original 1917 Articles 3, 27, 123, and 130 as recurring anchors for education, land and subsoil, labor, and Church-state relations.
- The distinction among legal recognition, administrative capacity, political commitment, and local implementation.
- The Plan of Ayala as an earlier agrarian standard against which constitutional and later government policy can be compared.

**Recommended materials.** Original constitutional facsimile and transcript; selected article excerpts; Plan of Ayala excerpt; a 1917 map and brief timeline; a Morelos land or village case; one short scholarly interpretation of the Constitution as both rupture and framework. Candidate anchors: [P1; P2; P3; C1; S1].

**Regional lens.** Morelos. The local case should show why a national land provision could not by itself resolve village claims, title disputes, hacienda power, or enforcement.

**Interpretive tension.** Constitution as social achievement versus Constitution as an instrument whose practical meaning emerged through later struggles.

**Progression link.** The chapter ends by asking who could legitimately interpret and govern the Revolution when constitutional authority, military power, regional support, and fulfillment of social promises did not align.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *promulgar*, *entrar en vigor*, *reconocer un derecho*, *hacer cumplir*, *propiedad*, *soberanía*, *sin embargo*. Concept word: *derecho*. Pattern: passive and impersonal **se** in legal and institutional language. Listening: a short modern Spanish explanation of what the Constitution established and what remained unresolved. Micro-output: *La Constitución reconoció..., pero su aplicación dependía de...*

**Editorial dependencies.** Verify that every excerpt comes from the original 1917 text; label modernized spelling, editorial insertions, and translations; do not present later amended text as original.

### Chapter 2. Who Spoke for the Revolution? Carranza, Zapata, and Competing Legitimacies

**Role in the arc.** Make legitimacy the central problem: constitutional office, military victory, popular support, local autonomy, and fulfillment of agrarian promises could support different claims to revolutionary authority.

**Central question.** In 1918-1919, was the Revolution best represented by a constitutional government, a continuing social movement, or an unresolved contest among rival revolutions?

**Historical backbone.**

- Carranza's constitutional government and the continuing limits of effective national control.
- Zapatista claims that land restitution and village authority were tests of revolutionary legitimacy.
- The difference between Article 27 as national law and the Plan of Ayala as a revolutionary agrarian program.
- Zapata's assassination in 1919 and the difference between the death of a leader and the disappearance of a movement or memory.
- The persistence of regional military and political networks despite constitutional government.

**Recommended materials.** Plan of Ayala; Article 27; a Carranza statement; a Morelos petition or local land record; newspaper or photographic coverage of Zapata; one scholarly comparison of national constitutionalism and Zapatista agrarian politics. Candidate anchors: [P1; P3; C2; C5; C6; S1; S5].

**Regional lens.** Morelos, with attention to village communities, sugar estates, land restitution, and the later political memory of Zapata.

**Interpretive tension.** Legal sovereignty versus revolutionary fulfillment. The chapter should not force a choice between one 'true' Revolution and all others; it should show how definitions of legitimacy produce different judgments.

**Progression link.** The unresolved problem of succession and coalition inside the Constitutionalist camp leads to the crisis of 1920.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *restituir*, *exigir*, *reclamar*, *apoyar*, *tierra*, *campesino*, *autoridad*. Concept word: *pueblo*, whose meaning may range from a town or village to 'the people' as a political collective. Pattern: *exigir que* and *pedir que* plus the subjunctive. Micro-output: attribute two different legitimacy claims using *según*.

**Editorial dependencies.** Select a community source with clear provenance; avoid using one village as representative of all of Morelos; explain that translations of *pueblo* may require contextual choices.

### Chapter 3. 1920: Agua Prieta and the Sonoran Ascendancy

**Role in the arc.** Show that 1920 changed the ruling coalition without automatically replacing military and personal networks with impersonal institutions.

**Central question.** What changed in 1920, and what remained unresolved?

**Historical backbone.**

- The succession conflict between Carranza and influential Sonoran leaders.
- The Plan of Agua Prieta of 23 April 1920 and its refusal to recognize Carranza's government.
- The rapid collapse of Carranza's coalition, his flight, and his death in May 1920.
- The provisional presidency of Adolfo de la Huerta and the election and accession of Alvaro Obregón.
- The political importance of Sonoran military, regional, and personal networks.

**Recommended materials.** Plan of Agua Prieta in Spanish and a reliable translation; map of Sonoran networks and military movement; competing statements from Carranza and the Agua Prieta coalition; newspaper coverage; short biographies of Obregón, Calles, and De la Huerta. Candidate anchors: [P4; C1; C6; S1; S8].

**Regional lens.** Sonora as a source of leadership, networks, and governing practices rather than merely a place of origin for future presidents.

**Interpretive tension.** Constitutional defense, rebellion, coup, coalition realignment, and revolutionary transition are overlapping but not identical descriptions.

**Progression link.** Once the Sonoran coalition holds national office, the problem shifts from defeating rivals to demobilizing forces, securing recognition, and governing a damaged country.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *desconocer*, *reconocer*, *sucesión*, *levantarse contra*, *derrocar*, *asumir el poder*, *legitimidad*. Concept word: *desconocer* in political usage - to refuse recognition or deny legitimate authority, not simply 'not know.' Pattern: *tras*, *a raíz de*, and *al + infinitive* for historical sequencing and causation. Micro-output: explain why translating *desconocer al gobierno* literally would miss the political claim.

**Editorial dependencies.** Compare the Spanish original with the English diplomatic translation; distinguish what the plan declared from what its coalition actually controlled.

### Chapter 4. Obregón and the Problem of Reconstruction

**Role in the arc.** Reframe reconstruction as a sequence of bargains among armed leaders, regions, workers, peasants, educators, creditors, and foreign governments rather than as a simple return to normality.

**Central question.** Was stabilization a necessary condition for revolutionary reform, a method of limiting the Revolution, or both?

**Historical backbone.**

- Demobilization, military loyalty, and continuing reliance on revolutionary generals and regional commanders.
- Selective accommodation of labor and agrarian constituencies.
- Creation of the Secretaría de Educación Pública in 1921 and the expansion of a national cultural and educational project.
- Financial reconstruction, transportation and administrative problems, and the need for reliable revenue.
- U.S.-Mexico relations, the Bucareli negotiations, diplomatic recognition, property claims, and the tension between sovereignty and external recognition.
- The De la Huerta revolt as an optional deeper case demonstrating continued succession and military fragility.

**Recommended materials.** Obregón statement; SEP creation and early program; diplomatic correspondence or a Bucareli summary grounded in primary records; worker or agrarian petition; infrastructure or fiscal graphic; source on the De la Huerta rebellion if included. Candidate anchors: [P5; C1; C2; C6; S1; S8].

**Regional lens.** Use Sonora for elite networks and one contrasting local case to show the uneven reach of reconstruction.

**Interpretive tension.** Compromise can be both an enabling condition for later reform and a restriction on immediate revolutionary demands.

**Progression link.** Political reconstruction alone cannot create a shared national identity. The next chapters examine education, murals, teachers, and cultural policy as additional forms of state-building.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *negociar*, *estabilizar*, *reconocimiento*, *concesión*, *compromiso*, *rebelión*, *reconstrucción*. Concept word: *compromiso*, which may imply commitment or compromise depending on context. Pattern: *aunque*, *sin embargo*, and *por un lado... por otro lado...* for qualified argument. Micro-output: state one way stabilization enabled reform and one way it limited it.

**Editorial dependencies.** Bucareli is often simplified in popular accounts; use diplomatic documents and careful scholarship rather than repeating claims about a single binding 'treaty' without qualification.

## Act II. Making and contesting revolutionary Mexico

### Chapter 5. Murals, Schools, and the Invention of Revolutionary Mexico

**Role in the arc.** Show that the postrevolutionary project attempted not only to govern territory but also to narrate a national past, define citizens, and make public culture part of political reconstruction.

**Central question.** Could schools, murals, language, and public ritual create a shared revolutionary nation, and whose version of Mexico did they present?

**Historical backbone.**

- The SEP under José Vasconcelos and the expansion of literacy, libraries, cultural missions, and public education.
- Public mural commissions and the work of Diego Rivera, José Clemente Orozco, David Alfaro Siqueiros, and others.
- The representation of workers, peasants, Indigenous people, conquest, colonialism, religion, and revolution.
- Indigenismo and the tension between honoring Indigenous heritage and assimilating living Indigenous communities into a national project.
- Schoolbooks, print culture, corridos, public ceremonies, and photography as additional forms of historical narration.

**Recommended materials.** One carefully selected mural or set of panels; artist or SEP statement; schoolbook page; corrido or print; photograph from the Casasola archive; short scholarship on revolutionary memory and cultural politics. Candidate anchors: [P5; C3; C5; C7; S2; S5].

**Regional lens.** Mexico City as commissioning center, paired with evidence of how national imagery or school materials circulated beyond the capital.

**Interpretive tension.** Cultural democratization and official narrative-making can occur together. Muralism should not be reduced to a uniform propaganda program; artists, officials, and audiences did not always agree.

**Progression link.** The next chapter follows national educational ambitions into specific communities, where teachers and residents negotiated what the Revolution would mean locally.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *representar*, *simbolizar*, *identidad*, *nación*, *indígena*, *mural*, *memoria*. Concept word: *nación*. Pattern: relative clauses and visual-description language, including *lo que representa* and *la figura que aparece*. Micro-output: describe one visual claim and one omission in Spanish.

**Editorial dependencies.** Obtain adequate image rights and resolution; distinguish analysis of a particular panel from claims about an entire mural cycle; supply alt text and provenance.

### Chapter 6. When the State Reached the Village

**Role in the arc.** Shift from federal intention to local negotiation. The rural teacher becomes a lens on education, health, agrarian policy, gender, language, and competing authority.

**Central question.** Did rural schools extend citizenship and public services, impose a national culture, or become places where communities reshaped the revolutionary project?

**Historical backbone.**

- The rural teacher as educator, organizer, mediator, reporter, and sometimes political agent.
- Literacy, sanitation, agricultural improvement, civic ritual, anti-alcohol efforts, and other expectations placed on schools.
- Indigenous education, Spanish-language nationalization, indigenismo, and local language and identity.
- Community petitions, cooperation, adaptation, resistance, and the role of municipal and religious authority.
- Women as teachers, organizers, workers, peasants, and political participants.
- The distinction between the presence of a federal institution and effective state control.

**Recommended materials.** Teacher report; community petition or complaint; rural-school photograph; education circular; two regional case summaries; women’s organization or citizenship source. Candidate anchors: [C2; C3; C7; S2; S3; S4].

**Regional lens.** Puebla and Sonora provide a strong comparison through the scholarship on rural schools. Chiapas can serve as an optional branch emphasizing Indigenous politics and incomplete or delayed state formation.

**Interpretive tension.** State penetration is not the same as state control. Communities could use schools and teachers for their own purposes while resisting other parts of the national program.

**Progression link.** Education also placed the state inside conflicts over religion, family life, and community authority, leading directly into the Calles and Cristero chapters.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *comunidad*, *maestro rural*, *petición*, *autoridad local*, *alfabetización*, *informar*, *resistir*. Concept word: *comunidad* in relation to *pueblo*. Pattern: *según*, reported speech, and verbs of requesting such as *pidió que*. Micro-output: compare what a federal official intended with what a community requested.

**Editorial dependencies.** Do not treat a teacher's report as a transparent account of the community; identify the reporting relationship, incentives, and intended audience. Preserve distinctions among Spanish, Indigenous-language, and translated testimony.

### Chapter 7. Calles, Anticlericalism, and the Limits of State Authority

**Role in the arc.** Examine how constitutional rules became politically explosive when the state attempted to regulate worship, clergy, education, and public authority more aggressively.

**Central question.** How far could the revolutionary state reach into religious practice, education, family life, and local authority?

**Historical backbone.**

- Article 130 and other anticlerical constitutional provisions as legal background.
- The Calles administration, the 1926 enforcement regime commonly associated with the Calles Law, and conflict over clergy and public worship.
- Catholic hierarchy, priests, lay organizations, teachers, municipal officials, governors, and families as distinct actors.
- Regional variation in enforcement and response.
- The transition from constitutional and political confrontation to armed conflict.

**Recommended materials.** Article 130; enforcement regulation or official circular; pastoral or lay Catholic statement; local newspaper; municipal or teacher account; photograph of public worship, church closure, or protest. Candidate anchors: [P1; C3; C5; C6; C8; S6; S7].

**Regional lens.** Jalisco and the Bajio, paired with a contrasting region where enforcement or Catholic mobilization differed.

**Interpretive tension.** The chapter should not collapse the conflict into 'government versus Church.' It involved constitutional authority, local political control, education, family practice, regional networks, and coercion.

**Progression link.** The next chapter follows how these conflicts became an armed rebellion and how civilians, women, teachers, and local communities navigated constrained choices.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *clero*, *culto público*, *educación laica*, *prohibir*, *hacer cumplir*, *oponerse a*, *quedar prohibido*. Concept word: *laicidad*. Pattern: prohibition and obligation - *se prohíbe*, *queda prohibido*, *deberá*, *no podrá*. Micro-output: identify what is prohibited, who must comply, and which actor is left unstated.

**Editorial dependencies.** Confirm the exact legal text and jurisdiction of each regulation; avoid using 'Calles Law' as a substitute for explaining the relevant provisions and enforcement context.

### Chapter 8. The Cristero War from Below

**Role in the arc.** Move from constitutional conflict to lived wartime choices and regional violence, while showing that religious identity, land, local power, and state coercion overlapped.

**Central question.** Why did political conflict become armed rebellion in some regions, and what choices were available to people living inside the conflict?

**Historical backbone.**

- The Cristero War from 1926 to 1929 and its regional concentration.
- Cristero fighters, federal forces, clergy, lay organizations, women’s support networks, teachers, agraristas, and civilians.
- Boycotts, underground worship, intelligence and supply networks, military repression, rebel violence, and displacement.
- The relationship between religious allegiance and local political or agrarian conflict.
- The 1929 arrangements that ended formal hostilities and the persistence of conflict afterward.

**Recommended materials.** Rebel proclamation or letter; federal military or government report; woman or lay activist account; civilian or teacher testimony; photograph; map of fighting; document or explanation of the 1929 settlement. Candidate anchors: [C2; C3; C5; C6; C7; S6; S7].

**Regional lens.** Jalisco and the Bajio, with Michoacan as a strong comparison for the relationship among agrarian policy, popular movements, and religious politics.

**Interpretive tension.** Immediate legal and religious triggers should be distinguished from enabling conditions such as local authority, regional networks, agrarian conflict, coercion, and community defense.

**Progression link.** The formal settlement coincided with a larger succession and institutional crisis after Obregón's assassination, pushing revolutionary leaders toward a national party organization.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *levantamiento*, *tropas*, *rebelde*, *represión*, *acuerdo*, *testimonio*, *huir*. Concept word: *conflicto*. Pattern: preterite versus imperfect in testimony - established conditions versus disruptive events. Micro-output: narrate one background condition and one event that changed the situation.

**Editorial dependencies.** Use trauma-sensitive framing; avoid fictional choice systems that imply a correct or winning path; clearly identify when testimony is retrospective and when a modern voice reads a historical text.

## Act III. Institutions, mass politics, and Cardenismo

### Chapter 9. Obregón's Assassination and the Maximato

**Role in the arc.** Make the problem of formal and informal authority visible. The political system had to manage succession after the president-elect was assassinated without reopening large-scale factional war.

**Central question.** Where did political power reside between 1928 and 1934: in the presidency, in Calles, in the army, in governors, or in emerging institutions?

**Historical backbone.**

- Constitutional change permitting Obregón's return, his 1928 election, and his assassination before taking office.
- The provisional presidency of Emilio Portes Gil and the subsequent presidencies of Pascual Ortiz Rubio and Abelardo L. Rodriguez.
- Calles as the 'Jefe Máximo' and the retrospective label Maximato.
- The relationship among formal presidential authority, personal influence, military networks, governors, and party development.
- The connection between succession management and the creation of the PNR.

**Recommended materials.** Newspaper coverage of the assassination; Calles speech on the end of the era of caudillos or succession; presidency and succession chart; political cartoon; correspondence or contemporary commentary on Calles's influence; short scholarly interpretation. Candidate anchors: [C6; C8; S1; S8].

**Regional lens.** Use a national institutional view while retaining the role of governors and regional political organizations.

**Interpretive tension.** Formal institutions can become stronger while informal personal influence remains decisive. Institutionalization should not be treated as synonymous with democratization.

**Progression link.** The next chapter examines the PNR as an attempt to organize factions and succession within a national political structure.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *asesinato*, *reelección*, *sucesión*, *influencia*, *ejercer el poder*, *presidente interino*, *caudillo*. Concept word: *poder*. Pattern: *seguir + gerundio*, *dejar de*, and contrasts between formal and practical authority. Micro-output: distinguish who formally held office from who continued to exercise influence.

**Editorial dependencies.** Explain that 'Maximato' is an analytical period label; avoid implying that the three presidents were identical or entirely without agency.

### Chapter 10. The PNR: From Armed Competition to Managed Politics

**Role in the arc.** Examine how political conflict was moved into a national party structure and how predictability, participation, elite bargaining, and constrained competition developed together.

**Central question.** Did the PNR transform political conflict into institutions, or place institutional language around a new form of centralized control?

**Historical backbone.**

- Founding of the Partido Nacional Revolucionario in 1929 amid the succession crisis.
- Regional parties, governors, military leaders, revolutionary factions, and their relationship to the national organization.
- The 1929 presidential election and the opposition campaign of José Vasconcelos as a useful case in political competition and legitimacy.
- Party mechanisms for succession and conflict management.
- The Great Depression, economic pressure, labor, migration, and the context in which political consolidation occurred.

**Recommended materials.** PNR founding statement or program; diagram of constituent organizations; campaign poster or newspaper; Vasconcelista source; governor or regional party source; short scholarship on regional experiments and national party formation. Candidate anchors: [P8; C6; C8; S1; S8].

**Regional lens.** Southeastern regional socialist experiments can provide an optional branch into the origins and varieties of party organization; governors should remain visible as actors rather than mere local agents.

**Interpretive tension.** A system can reduce military rebellion and make succession more predictable while also restricting electoral competition and making participation dependent on recognized organizations.

**Progression link.** Cárdenas inherited this institutional framework but altered its balance, mobilized new social sectors, and displaced Calles's practical authority.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *partido*, *coalición*, *incorporar*, *organizar*, *consolidar*, *oposición*, *sucesión presidencial*. Concept word: *institución*. Pattern: *convertirse en* and *quedar + participle* for institutional change and resulting states. Micro-output: make one claim about increased predictability and one qualification about political competition.

**Editorial dependencies.** Use historically accurate party names and dates; do not label the PNR as the PRI. Verify claims about the 1929 election against multiple source types.

### Chapter 11. Cárdenas: Land, Labor, Education, and Mass Politics

**Role in the arc.** Bring together agrarian reform, labor organization, education, regional variation, party transformation, and presidential authority. This is the main chapter for testing whether reform and political incorporation were contradictory or mutually reinforcing.

**Central question.** Did Cardenismo fulfill the Revolution, reorganize it, or make reform dependent on a stronger presidency and state-recognized mass organizations?

**Historical backbone.**

- Cárdenas's accession in 1934 and his conflict with Calles and Callista officials.
- Removal of Calles-aligned officials and Calles's exile in 1936, reinforcing the presidency as the primary national center of power.
- Expanded agrarian redistribution, ejidos, and collective projects, with attention to regional variation and administrative limits.
- Labor organization, the CTM, strikes, state mediation, and the connection between worker power and political incorporation.
- Peasant organization, the CNC, socialist education, teachers, Indigenous policy, and women’s organizing.
- Transformation of the PNR into the sectorally organized PRM in 1938.

**Recommended materials.** Agrarian petition and land-grant document; labor or union source; socialist-education source; women’s organizing source; PNR-to-PRM material; two contrasting regional case studies. Candidate anchors: [P7; P8; C2; C3; C6; C8; S2; S3; S9; S10].

**Regional lens.** Use at least two cases. Strong combinations include La Laguna and Yucatan; Michoacan and Sonora; or one celebrated reform case paired with one case of resistance, compromise, or limited implementation.

**Interpretive tension.** Redistribution and incorporation should be analyzed separately and then related. A policy can increase land, bargaining power, organization, or political voice while also connecting beneficiaries to the presidency, party, or official confederation.

**Progression link.** Petroleum expropriation becomes the culminating case because it joins Article 27, labor conflict, foreign capital, public mobilization, nationalism, and presidential power.

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *reparto agrario*, *ejido*, *sindicato*, *organización campesina*, *reforma*, *movilizar*, *redistribuir*. Concept word: *reforma* in relation to *incorporación*. Pattern: *para que + subjunctive* and *lograr que*. Micro-output: explain both what a policy redistributed and how it connected participants to a national organization.

**Editorial dependencies.** Avoid presenting Cardenismo as uniformly successful or uniformly imposed; select regional scholarship deliberately; explain the institutional differences among PNR, PRM, CTM, and CNC.

### Chapter 12. Oil, Sovereignty, and the Meaning of the Revolution by 1940

**Role in the arc.** Use petroleum expropriation as a capstone that reconnects constitutional authority, labor, foreign companies, courts and administration, national sovereignty, public participation, and political symbolism.

**Central question.** Was petroleum expropriation primarily a fulfillment of constitutional sovereignty, a resolution of labor conflict, an assertion against foreign corporate power, a consolidation of presidential authority, or a combination?

**Historical backbone.**

- Foreign-owned petroleum production, labor organization, wage and working-condition conflict, and the institutional path of the dispute.
- The relationship between Article 27, labor decisions, company responses, and the government's justification for expropriation.
- Cárdenas's announcement of expropriation on 18 March 1938 and publication of the decree on 19 March.
- Worker, company, diplomatic, and public perspectives.
- Public contributions and the later national memory of petroleum expropriation.
- The PRM and the broader political context of 1938-1940.

**Recommended materials.** Article 27 excerpt; labor award or reliable institutional summary; expropriation decree; Cárdenas speech or announcement; worker and company perspectives; diplomatic correspondence; photographs or public-contribution materials; Gulf Coast map. Candidate anchors: [P1; P6; C2; C3; C4; C6; C7; S1].

**Regional lens.** Veracruz and the Gulf Coast, paired with Mexico City as the site of national announcement and public mobilization.

**Interpretive tension.** National unity is part of the event's political representation, not an assumption. The chapter should distinguish labor, legal, economic, diplomatic, and symbolic explanations and allow them to operate together.

**Progression link.** The capstone returns to the Chapter 1 question: which constitutional promises had become administratively and politically actionable by 1940, and at what costs?

**Spanish integration.** Core terms: *expropiar*, *laudo*, *industria petrolera*, *recurso*, *soberanía*, *acatar*, *nación*, *indemnización*. Concept word: *soberanía*. Pattern: collective pronouns and modal or justificatory rhetoric - *nosotros*, *nuestro*, *deber*, *tener que*, *en defensa de*. Listening: a short Spanish passage from the announcement or a modern reading clearly identified as such. Micro-output: rank two explanations and justify the ranking with *debido a*, *sin embargo*, and *esto sugiere que*.

**Editorial dependencies.** Audit the exact labor and corporate sequence before scripting; distinguish the decree from later commemoration; identify modern readings as modern; clear rights for audio and imagery; avoid repeating simplified claims about universal public consensus.

# 5. Materials, source strategy, provenance, and bibliography

## 5.1 Recommended material layers

Each chapter should combine several kinds of material. The exact quantity is an experience-design choice, but the historical balance matters.

| Layer | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Narrative spine | Provide chronology, causal explanation, transitions, and orientation across actors and regions. |
| Primary evidence | Let the user investigate claims through laws, speeches, petitions, reports, newspapers, photographs, maps, art, or testimony. |
| Regional or local case | Test national claims at a smaller scale and reveal negotiation, unevenness, and actors outside Mexico City. |
| Historiographical interpretation | Make competing explanations visible and identify differences in evidence, scale, or definition. |
| Visual or audio material | Support source analysis and place-based understanding, not merely decoration. |
| Spanish layer | Use original wording, listening, concept terms, and rhetoric to deepen access to the history. |

## 5.2 Suggested source-packet formula

A compact, balanced packet is preferable to a large undirected archive. A useful default is:

1. One official or legal source, such as a constitutional article, decree, party statement, government report, or diplomatic document.
2. One source from an affected community or organized group, such as a petition, letter, union statement, teacher report, religious account, or testimony.
3. One visual or audio source, such as a photograph, mural detail, print, map, corrido, newspaper page, film clip, or recording.
4. One source tied to a specific state, municipality, village, workplace, or community.
5. One concise scholarly interpretation that explains the central historiographical problem.
6. One source or interpretation that complicates the most obvious conclusion.

Not every chapter needs exactly six items, and a single item may fill more than one role. The formula is a balance check, not a requirement.

## 5.3 Selection criteria

- Historical relevance to the chapter question, not merely ease of access or ease of Spanish.
- Clear provenance and enough metadata to establish creator, date, place, audience, and collection.
- Potential to support close reading or visual analysis rather than functioning only as an illustration.
- Ability to place official claims beside local, social, regional, or opposing perspectives.
- Manageable excerpt length while preserving enough context to avoid distortion.
- Rights or terms of use compatible with the intended deployment, or a fallback plan using links and thumbnails rather than reproduction.
- Accessibility potential, including transcript, alt text, caption, zoom, and readable contrast.

## 5.4 Recommended source metadata

| Field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Title and stable identifier | Allows citation, deduplication, and later retrieval. |
| Creator or issuing body | Supports analysis of authority, position, and bias. |
| Date and place | Places the source within the chronology and regional map. |
| Source type and genre | Law, petition, speech, newspaper, photograph, testimony, mural, report, and so forth. |
| Original language | Needed for bilingual display and translation workflow. |
| Intended audience | Helps distinguish public justification, internal reporting, private correspondence, and retrospective testimony. |
| Archive, collection, box or inventory | Preserves provenance beyond a web URL. |
| Complete or excerpted | Prevents an excerpt from being mistaken for the whole source. |
| Editorial changes | Records modernization, omissions, supplied words, or corrected spelling. |
| Translation and paraphrase status | Keeps original, translation, and learner support distinct. |
| Rights and permitted use | Determines whether the project can reproduce, crop, narrate, or only link to the item. |
| What the source can establish | States the evidentiary value of the item. |
| What the source cannot establish | Prevents public rhetoric or one local case from being overgeneralized. |

## 5.5 Provenance, rights, and translation rules

- Store the archival or institutional source record, not only a downloaded image filename.
- Check rights item by item. A collection's public search interface does not automatically grant unrestricted republication.
- Keep a link-only fallback for items that cannot be reproduced in the application.
- Distinguish archival audio from a modern reading; identify the voice, date, and nature of the recording.
- Do not silently normalize historical spelling, punctuation, or legal language. Provide a separately labeled modern-Spanish paraphrase when useful.
- Treat legal, diplomatic, and politically consequential translations as editorial work requiring review, not as an unverified automated transformation.
- When a word such as *pueblo*, *derecho*, *orden*, or *desconocer* has several plausible senses, record the translation decision and explain why it matters.
- Do not reproduce long copyrighted scholarly passages. Use concise quotations within applicable limits, paraphrase the argument, and cite the work.

# 6. Spanish-language learning recommendations

## 6.1 Priority hierarchy

The project should remain **history first**. Spanish is most effective when it improves access to Mexican sources, builds the language of historical explanation, and helps the user notice how political language constructs authority. Spanish tasks should not force a sophisticated historical conclusion into language that is too elementary for the user to express it.

1. Historical understanding and evidence-based interpretation.
2. Access to original Mexican voices and source genres.
3. Recognition of recurring historical and academic Spanish.
4. Short, manageable production in Spanish.
5. Optional extended language practice for the user who wants it.

## 6.2 Separate historical reasoning from Spanish output when needed

A chapter can produce two related outputs. The user may make the full historical argument in English, then express one manageable component in Spanish. For example:

> Historical interpretation: Article 27 created a constitutional basis for intervention in land ownership, but its effects depended on later political commitment, administrative capacity, and local contests over authority.

> Spanish micro-output: La Constitución reconoció nuevos derechos, pero su aplicación dependía de la capacidad y de las decisiones del gobierno.

As proficiency grows, the user can choose to move more of the historical work into Spanish. This should be optional and reversible by source or segment.

## 6.3 Suggested support modes

| Mode | Recommended behavior |
| --- | --- |
| History-first | Main narration in English; important quotations and terms in Spanish; Spanish-first source display with immediate support; five to six active language targets; all historical analysis possible in English. |
| Guided bilingual | Some narration and transitions in Spanish; Spanish transcript first; English translation on demand; more listening and short synthesis in Spanish. |
| Spanish-forward | Substantial narration and source analysis in Spanish; English available as support; Spanish recap and synthesis; historical glossary and translation notes remain accessible. |

## 6.4 Recommended explicit language load per chapter

- Five to eight core words or expressions worth reviewing across chapters.
- One historically important concept word whose meaning cannot be reduced to a single English equivalent.
- One grammar or rhetorical pattern found in the chapter's sources or narrative.
- One short listening activity using natural Spanish and repeated support.
- One brief written or spoken Spanish output.
- Two or three previously learned items resurfaced in a new context.

Most difficult words in a source should receive clickable support without automatically entering active review. A legal passage can contain many unfamiliar terms while only a small number are worth retaining.

## 6.5 Vocabulary categories

| Category | Examples | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core historical Spanish | *promulgar*, *derrocar*, *negociar*, *consolidar*, *hacer cumplir*, *dar lugar a* | Recycle across chapters and include in selective review. |
| Concept terms | *pueblo*, *nación*, *campesino*, *ejido*, *orden*, *revolución*, *soberanía* | Use concept cards with source occurrences, possible translations, and political context. |
| Source-support vocabulary | Specialized legal, clerical, military, or archival terms needed for one document | Gloss in place; do not automatically create flashcards. |
| Historical discourse | *a raíz de*, *a pesar de*, *mientras que*, *según*, *esto sugiere que* | Use in recaps and short analytical outputs. |

## 6.6 Grammar and rhetoric as historical interpretation

The most valuable grammar targets are patterns that affect how a source presents agency, causation, time, obligation, or collective identity.

| Pattern | Historical value | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive and impersonal *se* | Shows how legal and institutional language foregrounds a rule or action while leaving the actor unstated. | *Se promulgó la Constitución. Se reconocieron nuevos derechos.* |
| Preterite and imperfect | Distinguishes ongoing conditions from events that disrupted them, especially in testimony and narrative. | *La comunidad dependía...; llegaron las tropas...* |
| Purpose with *para que* | Clarifies official purpose while allowing comparison with actual outcome. | *El gobierno apoyó la organización para que los campesinos tuvieran representación.* |
| Prohibition and obligation | Reveals how laws address conduct and distribute authority. | *Se prohíbe...; queda prohibido...; deberá...; no podrá...* |
| Reported speech and attribution | Supports source criticism and prevents a statement from becoming an unqualified fact. | *Según la petición...; el informe afirmó que...* |
| Collective pronouns and national rhetoric | Makes inclusion, exclusion, and the construction of a national 'we' visible. | *Nosotros, nuestro pueblo, la nación mexicana.* |

## 6.7 Layered primary-source presentation

1. **Original source.** Preserve the historical Spanish as printed or transcribed, with editorial interventions disclosed.
2. **Glossed original.** Keep the text unchanged while providing contextual definitions, lemmas, pronunciation, grammar, and historical senses.
3. **Contemporary-Spanish paraphrase.** Offer a shorter, more accessible restatement that is clearly labeled as a paraphrase, not as the source.
4. **English translation.** Support line-by-line comparison and identify the translator or method of preparation.
5. **Translation note when needed.** Explain politically meaningful choices involving terms such as *pueblo*, *derecho*, *orden*, *desconocer*, or *reparto*.

These layers should be separate content objects. A user should never be uncertain whether a sentence is original, modernized, paraphrased, or translated.

## 6.8 Listening design

For selected short passages, use a three-pass structure:

1. Natural-speed audio without transcript. Ask for purpose, central contrast, tone, or gist rather than full transcription.
2. Spanish transcript with sentence replay, target expressions, optional moderate slowdown, and contextual glosses.
3. English translation and historical-rhetorical analysis, including why particular wording or pronouns matter.

Longer chapters may also include a three- to five-minute Spanish recap. The recap should restate the main developments in accessible Spanish rather than mechanically translate the English narration.

## 6.9 Spanish outputs and feedback

Useful outputs are brief, cumulative, and historically meaningful:

- One-sentence historical claim with a contrast or qualification.
- Paraphrase of one dense source sentence in contemporary Spanish.
- Comparison of official intention and local response.
- A sixty-second spoken recap: what happened, why it mattered, and what remains uncertain.
- Source attribution using *según* or a reporting verb.
- Evidentiary qualification: *Este documento muestra que..., pero no demuestra que...*

Feedback should prioritize: historical accuracy and intended meaning; intelligibility; one or two high-value corrections; and an optional more natural model. The system should not replace the user's historical idea with a polished but generic answer or bury it under minor corrections.

## 6.10 Selective review, recurring terms, and word biographies

Spaced review should be selective and contextual. Strong card types include phrase recognition, contextual cloze, political-sense recognition, connectors, and grammar recognition. Avoid open prompts with many equally valid answers unless the acceptable alternatives are explicit.

A particularly strong longitudinal feature is a **word biography**. The user follows one politically important word across sources, speakers, dates, and genres. Recommended candidates are *revolución*, *pueblo*, *nación*, *orden*, *derecho*, *tierra*, and *soberanía*. Each occurrence can record the source sentence, speaker, audience, likely meaning, translation choice, and political work performed by the term.

## 6.13 Language safeguards

- Do not simplify the historical argument to fit the user's Spanish production level.
- Do not silently modernize a primary source or present a paraphrase as original text.
- Do not choose a historically weak source merely because its Spanish is easy.
- Do not add every unknown word to review.
- Do not teach *ejido*, *pueblo*, *campesino*, *orden*, or *revolución* as simple one-to-one equivalents.
- Do not treat difficulty with archival or legal Spanish as proof of low general proficiency.
- Do not overcorrect spoken or written output; preserve the historical thought.
- Do not present a modern actor's reading as archival audio.

## 7.2 Important relationships

- A chapter can involve multiple themes, regions, people, organizations, events, and source items.
- One source item may have several excerpts, translations, paraphrases, audio readings, and language glosses.
- A vocabulary item should link to all source occurrences rather than duplicate disconnected entries.
- A historical claim should link separately to support, qualification, and counterevidence.
- A regional case should link back to the national policy it tests and forward to later chapters where the region reappears.
- A translation should never overwrite the original transcription.
- User notes or responses should not be treated as historical evidence, even when linked to a source.

## 7.3 Suggested minimum content package for a mature chapter

This package is a planning baseline, not a mandatory feature list:

- A central historical question and a concise statement of the chapter's role in the overall argument.
- Several narrative segments with a synchronized transcript and paragraph-level source notes.
- Four to six carefully chosen primary or visual source items, including at least one nonofficial or locally situated perspective.
- One regional or local scale shift.
- One explicit interpretive or historiographical dispute.
- One update to the timeline, promise ledger, power map, and living thesis.
- Five to eight active Spanish targets, one concept word, one grammar or rhetoric lens, one listening passage, and one micro-output.
- Complete provenance, rights status, translation status, and accessibility fields for every published asset.

# 8. Prototype recommendation and evaluation questions

## 8.2 Suggested prototype content package

- One complete chapter landing and orientation package.
- Forty-five to sixty minutes of segmented narration or a detailed narration script.
- Four to six verified source items with provenance and rights status.
- One regional or local case.
- One competing-interpretations component.
- One promise-ledger and living-thesis update.

## 8.3 Evaluation questions

- Does the historical narrative remain coherent when the user pauses for close source work?
- Does the source activity change or qualify the user's interpretation rather than simply repeat the narration?
- Can the user move between national and regional scales without losing orientation?
- Does the Spanish layer deepen access to the source without interrupting the historical argument?
- Can the user distinguish original text, paraphrase, translation, and commentary at all times?
- Does the cumulative notebook make changes in the user's interpretation visible?
- Are rights, provenance, and citation data sufficient to support later publication?
- Does the experience offer something meaningfully different from listening to a podcast or reading a book alone?

# 9. Editorial and research work still required

The coding agent should not assume that the existence of this reference means the content is publication-ready. The following tasks remain content dependencies.

1. Select exact excerpts and verify them line by line against authoritative scans or editions.
2. Confirm dates, names, institutional relationships, and quotations in the final narration with chapter-specific citations.
3. Choose exact regional cases and determine whether sufficient primary and scholarly material is available for each.
4. Audit rights and permitted uses for every image, audio clip, newspaper page, mural detail, map, and document scan.
5. Prepare Spanish transcriptions, translations, modern-Spanish paraphrases, glosses, and pronunciation support, with review appropriate to the source's difficulty.
6. Decide whether modern voice readings will be commissioned and how they will be labeled and credited.
7. Develop a consistent citation style and stable-source policy.
8. Obtain historical or subject-matter review for chapter scripts and captions.
9. Test accessibility: transcript quality, keyboard use, alt text, image zoom, color-independent labels, and readable bilingual layouts.
10. Determine which optional branches belong in the first release and which should remain future content.

## 9.1 Highest-priority content uncertainties

- The exact local Morelos case for Chapters 1-2.
- The exact community comparison for rural education in Chapter 6.
- The balance of civilian, women’s, teacher, federal, and rebel voices for Chapters 7-8.

# 10. Possible continuation after 1940

The first arc should end with a clear sense of continuation. A later version can preserve the same promise-to-outcome and regional framework.

| Possible later arc | Major problems and topics |
| --- | --- |
| 1940-1968: The Revolution becomes a regime | World War II, the Bracero Program, industrialization, urbanization, the Mexican Miracle, corporatism, migration, public health, mass media, agricultural modernization, inequality, and the 1968 student movement. |
| 1968-1982: Crisis of revolutionary legitimacy | Tlatelolco and memory, opposition, the Dirty War, populism, oil expansion, state violence, fiscal pressures, and the 1982 debt crisis. |
| 1982-2000: Reform and challenges to the revolutionary order | Market reform, the 1985 earthquake and civic mobilization, the 1988 election, privatization, constitutional and agrarian change, NAFTA, the 1994 Zapatista uprising, and the end of uninterrupted PRI presidential rule in 2000. |
